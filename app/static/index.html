    How it works

    The engine has two halves: a neural network that looks at a single position and has an opinion about it, and a search that uses those opinions to look ahead. Everything below runs live on this server.

    01

    Training data

    About 1.4 million positions, each labelled by Stockfish with its evaluation and best move. Roughly 180k come from master-level games (including recent games by Ding Liren and Gukesh), the rest from games played with randomised moves so the network sees unusual, unbalanced positions rather than only clean opening theory.

    02

    The network

    The board goes in as 18 planes of 8 by 8 (one per piece type and colour, plus side to move, castling rights and en passant). A residual convolutional trunk feeds four heads: a policy over all 20,480 from/to/promotion moves, a centipawn evaluation, the expected change in evaluation, and the expected game result.

    03

    The search

    Negamax with alpha-beta pruning and iterative deepening. The policy orders moves so cutoffs come early, and away from the root only the network's most likely moves, plus every capture, check and promotion, are explored. Leaf positions are sent to the network in batches, which is several times faster on a CPU than evaluating them one at a time.

    At the leaves, captures are played out before anything is evaluated, and the evaluation is the network's opinion plus a plain material count. The network on its own sometimes misjudges positions with pieces under attack, and anchoring it to material took the engine's score against a strength-limited Stockfish from 8% to 40%.

    04

    Self-play fine-tuning

    After supervised training, the engine plays itself. At every move Stockfish scores the engine's five favourite candidates and the best of them becomes the training target. A quarter of the time the engine deliberately plays one of the weaker candidates, so it also learns to punish and survive mistakes, and only games that end in checkmate are kept.
